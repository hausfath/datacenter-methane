Data-center power · quick estimate · 30 Oct 2026

What does off-grid gas power cost a data center?

At a 300 MW islanded site, with enough spare units for four-nines reliability and today’s equipment prices, gas power costs about $126/MWh from engines and $150/MWh from turbines, averaged over 20 years. That is 12–17% above delivered PJM wholesale power for engines and 33–40% for turbines. In Texas it is 1.5–1.8× the average industrial retail price.

PJM’s capacity term assumes the site draws at peak during PJM’s summer peaks ($17.1/MWh; $13.7 if not). Its energy and transmission terms are system load-weighted averages, which overstate what a flat load pays, so the off-grid premiums over PJM are probably floors. ERCOT wholesale leaves out transmission and delivery charges that every large load pays, so Texas retail is the more realistic bound.

What moves the number

One input changed at a time from the 300 MW central case. Spare units and the plant’s write-off period matter most. Gas price paths and financing move the answer by about ±10%.

Why spare capacity costs turbines more

Each design gets the fewest units for which the chance that enough are available to carry peak load is at least 99.99%. Each unit is assumed to be available 95% of the time (engines and turbines), independently of the others. Small units need few spares, so 300 MW takes 82 engines for 69 needed (+19%). Turbines of 35 MW need many more: 14 for 9 (+56%). The premium shrinks as sites get bigger.

Plants running today vs. ordered today

The central case prices a plant ordered now. Most machines running at data centers today were bought in 2023–25, closer to the low end of the capex range: about $114 for engines and $130 for turbines.

Many sites lease bridge power rather than own it. The only public anchor we found is Solaris Energy Infrastructure’s Q2 2026 power segment: $158M of revenue on about 950 MW, or roughly $56/kW-month. With fuel bought by the customer, that is about $129/MWh in year 1. If the spare turbines are leased too, it is $182/MWh. This is a fleet revenue average, not a contract rate.

Assumptions

Capex is 2026$ overnight and reflects current order-book prices. Brattle and Sargent & Lundy’s draft ERCOT cost-of-new-entry study (Oct 2026) puts a large frame turbine at $1,853/kW, up 51% in 18 months. For the smaller frames, aeroderivatives and engines that data centers use, Brattle says “recent estimates for these approach or exceed $3,000/kW overnight costs.” EIA’s AEO2026 figures predate that rise and serve as the low case.

Caveats

  • JudgmentEngine and turbine central capex rests on one Brattle statement about grid-connected peakers, applied here to both technologies. No primary source gives an installed $/kW for an islanded data-center plant.
  • JudgmentThe fuel-cell low case ($3,000/kW) and stack replacement (15% of capex every 5 years) are our assumptions. We found no verified Bloom contract price.
  • UnverifiedOutside estimates seen only in search snippets: RaboResearch (June 2026), ~$107/MWh for engines; Modo Energy, an “illustrative” $125/MWh; Black Hills, $1.8B for 564 MW of gas for Google’s Cheyenne campus (~$3,200/kW).
  • The reliability model is simple. Failures are independent and load sits at peak. Real sites share backup with UPS and diesel, and most need batteries for fast AI load swings (about +$8/MWh).
  • The grid comparison leaves out the interconnection costs large loads increasingly pay and the years-long wait for service. That wait is why sites go off-grid. Grid prices are also likely to rise, since ERCOT’s draft cost of new entry doubles to $290/kW-yr.
  • The 30% fuel-cell ITC (§48E(j)) and the 100% bonus depreciation are based on secondary legal summaries. Bonus depreciation only helps an owner with taxable income.
